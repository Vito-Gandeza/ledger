Market watch

Words you'll see

Plain-language meanings for every term on this page and in Gotrade.
Deposit

Moving pesos from your bank into Gotrade. Gotrade keeps ₱45 and turns the rest into dollars at its own rate. The dollars sit as cash — not invested yet.

Buy

Using Gotrade cash to buy shares of a fund. This is the step that invests. Cash on Gotrade does not grow; shares do.

Share

One small piece of a fund or company. If the fund's price rises 10%, every share you own is worth 10% more.

Fractional share

Part of a share. VOO costs ~$700 a share, but Gotrade lets you buy $10 of it and own 0.014 of a share.

Dividend

A small cash payment a fund makes from its companies' profits, about every 3 months (VOO: ~1.2% a year). It lands as Gotrade cash with US tax already taken. Buy more with it.

Sell

Turning shares back into Gotrade cash. Only needed when you want money out — selling in a dip locks in the loss.

Withdraw

Moving dollars from Gotrade back to your bank as pesos. Costs $5 plus Gotrade's exchange rate.

Fund / ETF

A basket of many stocks you buy as one ticker. VOO, VTI, SPY and QQQM are ETFs (exchange-traded funds).

Index fund

A fund that simply holds every company in a list, like the S&P 500. No one picks stocks, so fees are tiny and you get the market's return.

Ticker

A fund's short name on the stock market: VOO, VTI, AAPL. It's what you search for in Gotrade.

Compounding

Your growth earns growth. ₱10,000 up 8% becomes ₱10,800; next year the 8% is on ₱10,800. Slow at first, then it does more work than your deposits.

Dollar-cost averaging

Investing the same amount on a schedule no matter the price. You automatically buy more shares when prices are low.

Expense ratio

A fund's yearly fee, taken from inside the fund. VOO's 0.03% is ₱3 a year per ₱10,000.

Exchange spread

The gap between the real peso–dollar rate and the rate Gotrade gives you — about 2% each way. A hidden fee on deposits and withdrawals.

52-week high

The highest price in the past year. A fund well below it is "on sale" compared with recently.

Gain / loss

Value now minus what you paid. It only becomes real money when you sell — a dip you hold through isn't a loss you've taken.

Why index funds (VOO, VTI, SPY)

An index fund buys every company in an index, so one share spreads your money across hundreds of businesses. You don't need to pick winners — you own the market and earn the market's return, minus a tiny fee.

VOO and SPY both track the S&P 500 (the 500 largest US companies). They move almost identically; VOO's yearly fee is 0.03% vs SPY's 0.09%, so for buy-and-hold VOO is the cheaper copy. VTI holds the whole US market (~3,500 companies) at 0.03%; it's about 85% the same as VOO. Owning both adds little. Pick one as your core.

The S&P 500 has averaged roughly 10% a year since 1957 (about 7% after US inflation), but with drops of 20–50% along the way. The average only works if you stay in through the drops.

Buying regularly vs. buying the dip

Putting in a fixed amount on a schedule (dollar-cost averaging) buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when high, automatically. It also removes the hardest part — deciding when.

Waiting for a dip usually loses: markets spend most of their time near highs, and cash sitting out while you wait misses the gains. The "dip tilt" in settings only shifts where each scheduled deposit goes (toward a fund that's down from its high); it never delays buying.

Single stocks can fall 50–90% and never recover — that happens to index funds far less. If you want some, cap them at a small slice (say 10%) and keep the core in an index fund.

What Gotrade actually costs you

Deposit fee: ₱45 flat. On ₱1,000 that's 4.5%; on ₱6,000 it's 0.75%. Since it's flat, fewer and bigger deposits cost less — see "Deposits & fees" for the math on your numbers.

Exchange rate: Gotrade's rate is worse than the mid-market rate, both ways (about 2% each way when this was built). That's a ~4% round trip, which is why you buy and hold instead of moving money in and out.

Withdrawal: $5 per withdrawal, plus the spread.

Taxes and other things to know

US funds hold back tax on dividends for non-US investors (commonly 30%, or 25% under the US–Philippines treaty if a W-8BEN is on file). VOO yields around 1.2% a year, so this costs a few tenths of a percent a year. Log dividends after tax.

US estate tax can apply to non-US investors holding over $60,000 of US assets. That's years away at this pace, but worth knowing about later.

Keep an emergency fund outside the market (the cash buffer in settings). Selling in a crash to pay a bill is the one way to really lose money in an index fund.
